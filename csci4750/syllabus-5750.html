Course Description

This course introduces the field of machine learning, with emphasis on the probabilistic models that dominate contemporary applications. You will learn how computers learn from examples and extract patterns hidden in large data sets: classification algorithms that predict discrete labels, regression algorithms that predict continuous values, and both supervised and unsupervised settings — labeled training data available or not. The course stresses the conceptual relationships between these learning problems and the statistical models and computational methods behind them.

The course is about fundamentals: every method is derived, worked by hand on small numbers, and implemented from scratch before you use a library version. It is also about now: each lecture closes by showing where its classical idea lives in 2026–27 industry and research — the softmax regression inside a language model, the nearest-neighbor search behind recommendations, the gradient boosting inside fraud detection, the diffusion models behind image generators, the reinforcement learning behind reasoning models. The arc runs from k-nearest neighbors in week one to foundation models in April, measured all semester on one shared benchmark.

The course is lecture-oriented, with four substantial programming assignments, an in-class midterm, and a final project (teams of 1–4) presented on the three presentation days.

Audience & Prerequisites

CSCI 5750 is designed for M.S. students in Computer Science and related programs; advanced undergraduates take the same course under the CSCI 4750 listing.

  • Required: the undergraduate preparation behind the CSCI 4750 prerequisites (STAT 3850, CSCI 2100 Data Structures, MATH 2530 Calculus III). In practice: solid Python programming; probability and statistics at the level of a full statistics sequence; multivariable calculus.
  • Not assumed: linear algebra beyond the basics, NumPy, scikit-learn, or PyTorch. Lectures 3–4 are a self-contained math toolkit (linear algebra, gradients, optimization) taught for exactly what the course uses, and HW1 ships a NumPy warm-up and a setup check.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

  1. Formulate a prediction problem as learning from data — features, labels, hypothesis class, loss, and evaluation — and explain the difference between fitting the training data and generalizing.
  2. Derive and implement core supervised methods from scratch — nearest neighbors, linear and logistic regression, regularized models, Naive Bayes, decision trees — and explain how kernels, SVMs, and ensembles extend them.
  3. Connect losses to likelihoods: explain maximum likelihood, MAP estimation, and Bayesian inference, and use the probabilistic view to choose and justify a model.
  4. Apply unsupervised methods — k-means, Gaussian mixtures with EM, PCA, embeddings, matrix factorization — and interpret their output honestly.
  5. Build, train, and debug neural networks, from backpropagation by hand to convolutional networks in PyTorch, and explain how self-supervised learning, generative models, and reinforcement learning power today’s foundation models.
  6. Evaluate models rigorously — cross-validation, appropriate metrics, leakage checks, fairness criteria, and distribution shift — and recognize when a reported number should not be trusted.
  7. Conduct and communicate an original ML project: formulate a question, run experiments against baselines, and present findings in a written report and a talk.

Materials

All required materials are free.

  • [ISLP] James, Witten, Hastie, Tibshirani & Taylor, An Introduction to Statistical Learning with Applications in Python (Springer, 2023) — https://www.statlearning.com/ (primary text for Units 1–3; written for readers with a statistics background).
  • [PML] Kevin Murphy, Probabilistic Machine Learning: An Introduction (MIT Press,
    1. — https://probml.github.io/pml-book/book1.html (the probabilistic view, in more mathematical depth).
  • [MML] Deisenroth, Faisal & Ong, Mathematics for Machine Learning (Cambridge, 2020) — https://mml-book.github.io/ (for the math-toolkit lectures and PCA/GMM).
  • [UDL] Simon Prince, Understanding Deep Learning (MIT Press, 2023) — https://udlbook.github.io/udlbook/ (primary text for Unit 4).
  • [S&B] Sutton & Barto, Reinforcement Learning: An Introduction, 2nd ed. (2018) — http://incompleteideas.net/book/the-book-2nd.html (the two RL lectures).
  • Papers and posts for the “Now” segments and the modern-ML lectures (listed in the class schedule).
  • Reference courses if you want another angle: Stanford CS229, CMU 10-601, Berkeley CS189.

Compute: assignments are sized for Google Colab; nothing requires a paid account. A GPU helps only in HW4 and in some projects, and Colab provides one.

Course Structure

  • Unit 1 — Foundations: Learning from Data (Weeks 1–3, 8 lectures): the learning problem, nearest neighbors, the math toolkit, linear regression, generalization, regularization.
  • Unit 2 — Classification & Classic Supervised Learning (Weeks 4–7, 11 lectures + project studio + midterm): logistic regression, evaluation, generative classifiers, Bayesian inference, SVMs and kernels, trees and ensembles, learning theory, the practical workflow.
  • Unit 3 — Unsupervised Learning & Probabilistic Models (Weeks 8–11, 6 lectures): clustering, mixtures and EM, PCA, embeddings, recommender systems, graphical models.
  • Unit 4 — Neural Networks & Modern ML (Weeks 12–16, 13 lectures + presentations): neural networks and backpropagation, training, CNNs, attention and Transformers, self-supervised and foundation models, generative models, reinforcement learning, fairness and deployment, the frontier.

Policies

Attendance. Lecture is the core of the course and slides are not a substitute. Attendance is not directly graded, but in-class participation feeds the participation grade.

Late work. Each student has 5 free late days for the semester, at most 2 per assignment, applied automatically. Beyond that, −5 points per additional day late (partial days are rounded down). Late days do not apply to the midterm or to the project proposal, presentation, or final report (hard deadlines because they gate feedback and scheduling).

Collaboration. Discussing ideas is encouraged; sharing or copying code or written answers is not. Each submission lists everyone you discussed it with.

AI tools policy. Machine learning is the science behind today’s AI assistants, so using them thoughtfully is part of what you learn here, not a loophole. Three tiers, stated on every assessment:

  • Tier A — Required or freely permitted: designated parts (HW4 Part 3, and parts of many projects) ask you to use pretrained models, and AI assistants are permitted freely there, with disclosure.
  • Tier B — Permitted with disclosure (default for homework): you may use AI assistants for homework the way you’d use a colleague — to explain concepts, debug, or draft — provided
    1. you disclose the tool and how you used it, and (2) you can explain every line you submit. Instructors may ask you to walk through your solution; inability to explain it is treated as an integrity violation. Core “from scratch” functions flagged ★ in each assignment must be your own typing.
  • Tier C — Prohibited: the in-class midterm (closed book). In-class participation questions are answered by you, in the room, without AI tools — they are credited for participation, not correctness, so there is nothing to gain anyway.

The assessment mix (in-class exam + oral project presentation + explainability rule) is deliberately designed so the course measures what students can do, not what their tools can do.
