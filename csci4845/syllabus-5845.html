Course Description

Natural language processing (NLP) is the study of computational systems that understand and generate human language. This course is a comprehensive tour of the field across its three eras: classical NLP (n-gram language models, text classification, word vectors, sequence labeling, parsing), neural NLP (word embeddings, recurrent networks, attention, the Transformer), and the large language model era (pretraining, alignment and RLHF, prompting, efficient fine-tuning, retrieval-augmented generation, agents, evaluation, and safety). A central theme is that today’s LLMs are the direct descendants of the simplest model we build in week two — the language model — and the course follows that single thread from counting bigrams to reasoning models.

The course is lecture-oriented, with four substantial programming assignments, an in-class midterm, and a final team project presented during finals week.

Audience & Prerequisites

CSCI 5845 is designed for M.S. students in AI and CS; advanced undergraduates take the same course under the CSCI 4845 listing.

  • Required: solid programming ability in Python; comfort with probability, linear algebra, and calculus at the level of an undergraduate course; a prior machine learning course.
  • Not assumed: PyTorch experience. Lectures 11–12 include a self-contained neural network refresher (paced via the first-class background questions), and a week-1 setup guide walks through the tooling.

Learning Outcomes

By the end of the course, students will be able to:

  1. Build and evaluate classical NLP models — n-gram language models, Naive Bayes and logistic regression classifiers, HMM taggers — from scratch, and explain when simple methods remain the right tool.
  2. Explain the mathematical machinery of neural sequence models: embeddings, recurrence, attention, and the Transformer architecture, including subword tokenization.
  3. Implement and train a small Transformer language model, and fine-tune pretrained models for downstream tasks.
  4. Explain how modern LLMs are built end to end: pretraining data and scaling laws, instruction tuning, RLHF/DPO, and inference-time techniques.
  5. Apply the modern LLM toolkit — prompting, parameter-efficient fine-tuning, retrieval-augmented generation, and tool use/agents — and justify which approach fits a given problem and budget.
  6. Design rigorous evaluations for NLP systems, including benchmark pitfalls, human evaluation, and LLM-as-judge methods, and analyze systems for bias, hallucination, and safety failures.
  7. Conduct and communicate an original NLP project: formulate a question, run experiments, and present findings in a conference-style report and talk.

Materials

All required materials are free.

  • [SLP3] Jurafsky & Martin, Speech and Language Processing, 3rd ed. draft — https://web.stanford.edu/~jurafsky/slp3/ (primary text for Units 1–2 and parts of Unit 3; chapter numbers in the schedule follow the 2025 draft before the semester).
  • [E] Eisenstein, Introduction to Natural Language Processing — secondary reference with stronger mathematical treatment; free PDF at https://github.com/jacobeisenstein/gt-nlp-class/blob/master/notes/eisenstein-nlp-notes.pdf.
  • Papers and posts for the LLM-era lectures (linked in the class schedule); roughly one primary reading per lecture in Unit 3.
  • Reference courses if you want another angle: Stanford CS224N, CMU 11-711.

Compute: assignments are sized for Google Colab (free tier suffices; Pro is a convenient $10/mo upgrade). HW4 and some projects use LLM APIs — free local alternatives (Ollama, small open-weight models) cover every required task, so no paid account or API key is needed.

Course Structure

  • Unit 1 — Foundations & Classical NLP (Weeks 1–5, 10 lectures): text processing, n-gram LMs, classification, word vectors, sequence labeling, parsing.
  • Unit 2 — Neural NLP & the Transformer (Weeks 6–8, 5 lectures + midterm): neural nets, RNNs, seq2seq and attention, machine translation, the Transformer, tokenization.
  • Unit 3 — Pretraining & the LLM Era (Weeks 9–15, 11 lectures): BERT and GPT, scaling, alignment, prompting, efficiency, RAG, agents, evaluation, safety, frontier topics.

Midterm format. The full pool of possible exam problems is released Thu Oct 8, one week before the exam, and every exam problem is drawn from that pool. Work through it however you like — study groups and AI assistants included — but the exam itself is closed book: bring the solutions in your head, not on paper.

CSCI 5845 requirements (vs. CSCI 4845). The two listings share lectures, homework deadlines, the midterm, and the in-class participation questions. CSCI 5845 requirements differ from the undergraduate listing in two places:

  • Homework: each of HW1–HW4 includes a marked graduate extension (the “G part”) — a short additional experiment or analysis tied to a primary paper (see the assignments page). For CSCI 5845 students the G part is required and worth 10 of the 100 points on each homework (core parts are scaled to 90).
  • Final project: teams including a CSCI 5845 student complete the research/reproduction track — including a related-work section and an experimental comparison against at least one baseline (see the project page). An application-track proposal is allowed only with instructor approval and an unusually rigorous evaluation plan.

Policies

Attendance. Lecture is the core of the course and slides are not a substitute. Attendance is not directly graded, but in-class participation feeds the participation grade.

Late work. Each student has 5 free late days for the semester, at most 2 per assignment, applied automatically. Beyond that, 10% off per day, and no submissions more than 4 days late. Late days do not apply to the project proposal, presentation, or final report (hard deadlines because they gate feedback and scheduling).

Collaboration. Discussing ideas is encouraged; sharing or copying code or written answers is not. Each submission lists everyone you discussed it with.

AI tools policy. This is an NLP course — using language models thoughtfully is a course topic, not a loophole. Three tiers, stated on every assessment:

  • Tier A — Required: parts of HW4 and many projects require using LLMs/APIs.
  • Tier B — Permitted with disclosure (default for homework): you may use AI assistants for homework the way you’d use a colleague — to explain concepts, debug, or draft — provided
    1. you disclose the tool and how you used it, and (2) you can explain every line you submit. Instructors may ask you to walk through your solution; inability to explain it is treated as an integrity violation. Core “from scratch” functions flagged in each assignment must be your own typing.
  • Tier C — Prohibited: the in-class midterm (closed book). In-class participation questions are answered by you, in the room, without AI tools — they are credited for participation, not correctness, so there is nothing to gain anyway.

The assessment mix (in-class exam + oral project presentation + explainability rule) is deliberately designed so the course measures what students can do, not what their tools can do.
